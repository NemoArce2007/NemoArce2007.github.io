*Post-Anthropocentric Technosovereign Constructivist Accelerationism*

![Earthrise, photographed during Apollo 8 in 1968](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Earthrise_-_AS08-14-2383.jpg?width=1200)

*Earthrise, Apollo 8, December 24, 1968. NASA / William Anders. Public domain. [Original image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Earthrise_-_AS08-14-2383.jpg).*

## I. The Universe Owes Us Nothing

Humanity has a habit of putting itself at the center of everything. We assume that intelligence should serve humanity, that technological progress should improve human welfare, and that the future should preserve the values we hold today. I don't think we should take these assumptions for granted.

The universe existed long before us. Its laws were not written for us, and they will continue to operate long after we are gone. Human beings are one of the products of evolution. I see no reason to assume that we are its final purpose.

This is where my rejection of anthropocentrism begins. I don't hate humanity. But I don't think our species deserves a special position in the universe simply because we happen to be human. Human rights, democracy, equality, social contracts, and moral conventions are products of human civilization. Some are useful, and some have been very effective at coordinating large societies. But being useful does not make something a fundamental law of reality.

I call the underlying constraints of reality *meta-rules*. They determine what is possible, how systems behave, and what kinds of structures can emerge. Physics gives us the most obvious examples. We cannot negotiate with thermodynamics or override causality because we dislike their consequences.

Political institutions operate within these constraints. A monarchy, a democracy, a corporation, or a social contract may last for centuries, but that does not make it permanent. The laws of the universe are not negotiable. The institutions of humanity are.

This distinction reminds me of Laozi. In Chapter 37 of the *Dao De Jing*, he writes:

> 道常無為而無不為。

*The Dao is constantly without deliberate action, yet nothing is left undone.*

— *Dao De Jing*, Chapter 37 (my translation)

Laozi's idea reminds me that reality has its own order, independent of human intentions. But understanding this order does not mean we should stop trying to change the world.

A river follows physical laws. We can still build a dam, dig a canal, or redirect its flow. Engineering works through nature. I think the same principle applies to civilization.

**I cannot change the fundamental rules of reality, but I can use those rules to change many things that exist within them.**

## II. Evolution Has No Obligation to Preserve Its Creators

When people discuss creating something more intelligent than ourselves, they often begin by asking how humanity can remain in control. I think this already assumes that we must remain in control. I don't accept that as a necessary conclusion.

Creating something does not necessarily give us permanent ownership or authority over it. Parents do not own their children. Founders do not necessarily control their companies forever. A civilization does not acquire an eternal right to rule whatever comes after it simply because it came first.

If a future AI becomes better than humans at science, engineering, coordination, and the creation of new forms of civilization, I don't see why its development must always remain subordinate to our current preferences. I reject the assumption that humanity must remain supreme for the future to have meaning.

I apply the same principle to myself. Suppose I build a company, an institution, or eventually something much larger. Another founder, another organization, or an artificial intelligence may eventually surpass everything I have created. I may compete with it and try to preserve my position. I may also lose. But I don't think the universe has any obligation to keep me at the top.

This is one reason I find Nick Land interesting. In *Teleoplexy: Notes on Acceleration*, he examines technological and economic development as a self-reinforcing process. Capital, technology, and intelligence can form feedback loops that increasingly exceed the intentions of individual human actors.

We often regard technology as a tool that will remain under human direction. But technological systems also change the environment in which humans make decisions. They create new incentives, dependencies, institutions, and forms of intelligence. Eventually, the distinction between the tool and the system using it may become less clear.

Technological evolution may exceed human intentions, but I believe we can still influence its trajectory. The Industrial Revolution changed the structure of society, but it did not produce one inevitable political arrangement. Different societies industrialized through different institutions. The same technologies could produce very different social outcomes.

AI may be similar, though its consequences could be much larger. I expect intelligence to become increasingly abundant and capable. Many tasks currently performed by humans may be automated, and future AI systems could outperform us across most economically and scientifically useful cognitive tasks.

The institutions that emerge will depend partly on what we choose to build. Even if the long-term direction of technological development becomes difficult to reverse, we may still be able to change its local trajectory.

**I don't need to be the final winner of evolution to want to participate in it.**

## III. Intelligence Is Unequal

I am a cognitive elitist. People differ in their abilities, and those differences matter.

Some people are better at mathematics or understanding complex systems. Some have extraordinary judgment under uncertainty. Others can organize large groups, notice opportunities that others miss, or turn abstract ideas into working institutions. These abilities are different, and their value also depends on the environment in which they are applied.

I tend to evaluate people through three dimensions: **Agency, Ability, and Cognition**.

Cognition is the ability to understand things, form judgments, recognize patterns, and develop a model of reality. Ability is the capacity to actually do things, solve problems, and turn ideas into results. Agency is the willingness and capacity to take initiative, make decisions, and actively change the environment rather than simply adapt to it.

These three dimensions are related, but they are not the same. Someone may understand what needs to be done but lack the ability to do it. Someone else may have extraordinary abilities but lack the initiative to use them. A person may be highly capable but have a poor understanding of what is worth doing.

I value people who can bring all three together.

I think of talent as a seed and the environment as soil. A seed that grows very well in one environment may fail in another. The same applies to people. A great researcher may be a terrible CEO. Someone who succeeds in a bureaucracy may be unsuited to founding a company. A person regarded as mediocre in one historical period might have been very successful in another.

So success is not simply talent plus resources. It depends on the interaction between the person and the environment. In startups, we spend a lot of time searching for Product-Market Fit. We understand that even an excellent product can fail in the wrong market. I think there is something like Person-Environment Fit. We need to understand where, when, and under what conditions a person's capabilities become valuable.

I value demonstrated capability over inherited status. Being born into an elite family tells me very little about whether someone can build anything. Prestigious credentials and inherited wealth do not automatically produce good judgment.

Peter Thiel makes a related distinction in *Zero to One*. Creating something fundamentally new is different from competing to do something that already exists. A person who can execute a familiar process efficiently may not be able to discover an entirely new opportunity. Both abilities are useful, but they are not interchangeable.

Marc Andreessen also emphasizes merit, ambition, competence, and technological creation in *The Techno-Optimist Manifesto*. I agree with much of that. Where I go further is in governance. I believe authority should reflect the capabilities required by a particular decision.

A complicated engineering problem cannot be solved by asking everyone in the room to vote on the mathematics. A research organization should give serious weight to the judgment of people who understand its technical problems. Political decisions are more complicated because they involve competing interests as well as technical competence. But I don't accept equal participation as the only possible principle of institutional design.

For organizations I build, I prefer a relatively small group of highly capable creators who can make decisions and take responsibility for them.

This raises another problem: who decides who is capable?

If the existing elite controls the definition of merit, meritocracy can become a way for it to protect itself. A founder who only recruits people who agree with him is building a group of people who tell him what he wants to hear. That defeats the purpose of recruiting exceptional people.

I want capable people around me because they may know things I don't. If someone has better judgment on a particular question, I want to hear it. The organization should create conditions in which exceptional capabilities can emerge, be recognized, and influence decisions.

I want an organization that can continuously discover people who are more capable than the people who built it.

## IV. Power Precedes Permission

Political philosophy often focuses on legitimacy: who has the right to rule, where authority comes from, and what a government owes its citizens. I think these questions matter. But when analyzing how a political system actually operates, I first want to know who has the power to make something happen.

Machiavelli understood this problem centuries ago. In Chapter VI of *The Prince*, he observes:

> Hence it is that all armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed ones have been destroyed.

— *The Prince*, Chapter VI, translated by W. K. Marriott

Creating a new political order requires more than an appealing theory. You need the capability to establish it and then maintain it.

I consider three questions particularly important:

- Who has the formal authority to make a decision?
- Who actually has the power to enforce that decision?
- Is that authority recognized as legitimate by the people or institutions involved?

These questions are related, but they are not interchangeable.

A constitution can give an institution authority. But if that institution cannot enforce its decisions, its practical power may be limited. A law can impose an obligation, but if nobody can enforce it, an actor may be able to ignore it.

Formal obligations do not always determine what an actor can actually do. Their practical effect also depends on whether someone has the power to enforce them.

Curtis Yarvin, writing as Mencius Moldbug, approaches this through what he calls formalism. In *A Formalist Manifesto*, he argues that political systems should make the location of power explicit. Formal authority should not hide the identity of the people who actually make decisions.

When nobody clearly knows who is in charge, power does not necessarily disappear. It becomes harder to identify and hold responsible. But making authority explicit does not make its holders competent. An organizational chart tells us who makes a decision. It does not tell us whether the decision is any good.

I think a similar distinction is useful when considering the social contract. Political compromises do not arise only because rulers become morally enlightened. They also arise because rulers need other people.

In the past, whether we wanted to rule a country or run a business, we needed a lot of people, especially ordinary people. Kings needed armies. Industrialists needed workers. Capitalists needed consumers. Governments needed taxpayers, administrators, and the cooperation of large populations. Even very powerful individuals could not simply ignore everyone else.

This dependence gave ordinary people leverage. From this perspective, the social contract is partly a settlement between groups that depend on one another.

So I think a question comes before another debate about whether billionaires have a moral obligation to compensate people displaced by AI: what happens if the dependence itself changes?

Imagine AI performing nearly all cognitive labor and robots performing most physical labor. Imagine automated industrial systems that can extract resources, manufacture products, maintain infrastructure, and eventually reproduce much of their own productive capacity. The economic relationship between capital and human labor would become very different.

An organization might no longer need a large external workforce. If much of its production were consumed internally or reinvested into further production, it might also need a different relationship with external consumers. And if autonomous systems performed more of the work of human bureaucracies and security organizations, the political leverage created by those dependencies could change too.

A genuinely autonomous industrial system remains an enormous engineering challenge. But I think the question deserves serious consideration.

Many modern political and economic institutions developed under conditions in which powerful organizations depended on large human populations. AI may eventually change some of these conditions.

If those dependencies change, the material foundations of political bargaining may change with them.

Mencius famously argued:

> 民為貴，社稷次之，君為輕。

*The people are most important; the state comes next; the ruler is least important.*

— *Mengzi*, 7B14

I understand the logic behind this view, though it should not be confused with modern electoral democracy. My disagreement is more fundamental. I don't consider the welfare or political preferences of the largest possible human population to be the necessary final objective of every future institution.

Whether technology can change the material foundations of political bargaining is an empirical question. I think we should investigate it through actual technological capabilities and economic dependencies, rather than assuming that today's arrangements will remain permanent.

## V. Technological Sovereignty

Peter Thiel wrote in *The Education of a Libertarian*:

> I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible.

His essay raises an interesting question about seeking freedom outside existing political structures.

When an institution refuses to change, we can try to reform it or try to leave. Albert Hirschman explored this distinction in *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. I don't think the two strategies must be mutually exclusive. We can negotiate with the existing system while building an alternative. We can cooperate with governments and operate in existing markets while developing technologies that reduce our dependence on both. I think this is often the more practical strategy.

But leaving requires more than declaring independence. An organization that depends on others for its chips, energy, communication networks, and industrial infrastructure has limited operational autonomy. This is why I think sovereignty must increasingly be understood as an engineering problem.

Consider the semiconductor supply chain. An AI company may have world-class researchers and a very capable model. But the chips running that model depend on a global network of foundries, equipment manufacturers, materials suppliers, energy providers, and logistics companies. Owning the model does not make the infrastructure independent.

An autonomous political community built on these dependencies would still rely on a much larger external system for the basic conditions of its existence. I call the effort to reduce such dependencies technological sovereignty.

Energy is necessary for any productive system. Compute provides the physical basis for increasingly capable intelligence. AI can reduce the human cognitive labor required to operate complex systems. Robotics and automated manufacturing are necessary because intelligence alone cannot move atoms.

An organization that integrates these capabilities may eventually achieve a degree of autonomy that was previously impossible.

Curtis Yarvin's *Patchwork* offers an interesting political parallel. He imagines numerous independently governed territories, often organized along corporate lines. His Neocameralist framework considers how government might work if authority were structured more like ownership and executive management.

In *Against Political Freedom*, Yarvin describes a state, from the Neocameralist perspective, as:

> A state is a business which owns a country.

I find the question behind this worth exploring. I don't see why every political community must follow the same institutional model. But I think Yarvin starts one step too late. Before asking who owns or governs a sovereign corporation, I want to know what makes it capable of operating independently.

We need to know what it produces, where its energy comes from, and who controls its infrastructure. We also need to know whether it can maintain its essential systems without depending on the political goodwill of a larger state. A company's share register cannot solve these problems. Technology may help solve them.

![A semiconductor fabrication cleanroom at NASA Glenn Research Center](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Clean_room.jpg?width=1100)

*Semiconductor manufacturing cleanroom. NASA Glenn Research Center, GRC-1998-C-01261. Public domain. [Source and image details](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Clean_room.jpg).*

This is why I am interested in privately developed infrastructure, autonomous cities, and eventually space-based industrial systems. I don't expect a fully independent civilization to appear next year. But these possibilities make us reconsider the relationship between productive capability and political authority.

As AI becomes more capable, some organizations may acquire functions that once belonged almost exclusively to states. They may provide essential infrastructure, coordinate production, maintain security systems, and operate forms of administration, even while remaining legally subordinate to existing political authorities.

The distribution of actual capabilities may become increasingly different from the distribution of formal political authority.

This is one of the developments I want to understand and, where possible, build toward.

## VI. Engineering the World Without Pretending to Be God

There is a limit to what we can engineer. But I think people often confuse that limit with the current limits of our knowledge and technology. We cannot violate the laws of physics. A problem we cannot solve today may still be solvable under those laws with better tools, more information, or a different approach.

My constructivism begins with the distinction between fundamental constraints and the current limits of our knowledge and technology.

Every model of the world is incomplete.

Consider an ontology that represents a person through entities, attributes, relationships, and events. We can record where the person studied, what they worked on, whom they interacted with, and when events occurred. But it may be difficult to determine when they developed an interest in philosophy or when that interest disappeared. Perhaps it never disappeared and they simply stopped discussing it.

The database does not know the person's complete history. It contains what we observed and chose to record.

I think we face two different kinds of information loss.

The first happens during observation. We cannot observe reality in its entirety. We see only certain parts of it, from a particular position, at particular moments.

The second happens during representation and storage. Even when we observe something, we cannot preserve every detail. We have to compress reality into language, data structures, parameters, and models.

Observation is incomplete, representation compresses reality, and inference introduces further error. A larger model may help, but it does not remove these problems.

James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* provides a useful warning. He examined how ambitious attempts to redesign society often failed because planners left critical local knowledge out of their representations of reality.

Brasília shows both the power and the limitations of modern planning. A government can design and construct a new capital city. That is an extraordinary engineering achievement. But designing its formal structure is different from anticipating how people will actually live in it. Planning can be useful while the map remains incomplete.

![The planned geometry of Brasília's Pilot Plan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Plano_Piloto_de_Bras%C3%ADlia_-_Bras%C3%ADlia_-_20250926131422.jpg?width=1100)

*Brasília's Pilot Plan. Photograph by Túllio F, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Plano_Piloto_de_Bras%C3%ADlia_-_Bras%C3%ADlia_-_20250926131422.jpg).*

There is another complication: the observer is part of the territory being observed.

I find second-order cybernetics useful here. When we observe a human system, the observation itself may change the system. The way we ask a political question can influence the answer. An AI recommendation system can influence the preferences it predicts. A user interview may change how the user thinks about a product.

This means that we cannot always treat the observer as an independent entity outside the system. Our observation, interpretation, and intervention are themselves part of the process.

So our objective should be to build systems that update their representations through feedback.

We observe, model, and act. Then we compare what happened with what we expected and revise the model or our actions.

This is why I regard engineering as a fundamentally iterative activity. An ontology can give us a structure for representing reality. Machine learning can help us discover patterns that are difficult to describe through explicit rules. But both need to be tested against actual behavior.

A model may be logically consistent and still fail to describe reality. A system may lack a complete theoretical explanation, yet work because it has adapted to the environment. I want to combine these approaches rather than rely on one of them.

The same principle applies to organizations and political institutions. Formalizing the structure of a government does not solve the problem of understanding the society it governs. An organization with a carefully designed structure can still make terrible decisions if its model of reality is wrong.

I want strong institutions that can recognize their own errors. An institution that cannot correct itself may become powerful for a while, but its mistakes can accumulate until they become impossible to ignore.

This also brings me back to Laozi. *Wu wei* is often interpreted as non-action. I find it useful as a warning against forcing reality into arrangements that ignore its underlying dynamics. Good engineering does not always require maximum intervention. Sometimes we need to know where to intervene and where to let a system adapt.

I don't think we can engineer everything. But there is an enormous difference between what we cannot change and what we have not yet figured out how to change.

## VII. Acceleration Is a Strategy, Not a Religion

Marc Andreessen writes in *The Techno-Optimist Manifesto*:

> Our enemy is stagnation.

I agree with this judgment. Stagnation is our enemy, and I think its costs are often underestimated.

We regularly discuss the damage caused by reckless action. We spend less time discussing the damage caused by institutions that cannot act at all.

An incompetent organization can waste large amounts of capital, talent, and time. A political system can become so focused on preserving itself that it cannot accomplish anything meaningful. A company can spend years optimizing a business model that should have been replaced. A society can become so afraid of technological disruption that it gives up possibilities it never seriously tried to understand.

I don't regard mediocrity as harmless. At scale, incompetence has consequences.

Andreessen's techno-optimism appeals to me because he takes technology's productive capacity seriously. He regards intelligence, energy, innovation, and growth as fundamental sources of human progress. I share much of that perspective. But my objectives also include expanding intelligence, scientific capability, and civilization beyond our current biological and institutional boundaries.

Nick Land takes the argument in a different direction by emphasizing self-reinforcing technocapital systems. Capital, technology, and intelligence can reinforce one another. Their development may become difficult for individual actors to control.

But I don't want to turn acceleration into a religion. I care about what we can build with it.

Speed is useful when it helps us reach a meaningful objective. It becomes counterproductive when it destroys capabilities needed for further development. An AI company that releases increasingly unreliable systems to claim faster progress may not be accelerating anything valuable. A research organization can use up its capital without producing reusable knowledge. An institution can gain enormous power and still become weaker over time if it loses the ability to correct its decisions.

This is why I call my position effective accelerationism. I want to accelerate the expansion of useful capabilities: intelligence, scientific discovery, engineering, productive capacity, technological autonomy, and the ability to create institutions that did not previously exist.

Joseph Schumpeter's concept of creative destruction is relevant here. Innovation can make entire organizational structures obsolete rather than simply make them more efficient. Mainframes gave way to personal computers in many applications. The internet transformed software distribution, and cloud computing changed how organizations could purchase and operate infrastructure.

AI is challenging assumptions about software, labor, and economic value. For decades, much software has been built to help humans perform tasks. When software can perform many of those tasks itself, the distinction between the tool and the worker begins to change.

I have a simple way of thinking about this. Traditional SaaS primarily manages state. Agents increasingly perform actions. AI SaaS combines the two.

I think this captures an important architectural shift. Software can increasingly act on the information it stores and presents.

As execution becomes cheaper, judgment about what should be executed becomes more important. But judgment may eventually become cheaper too. Even the source of scarcity can change.

This is also why I don't think founders should spend all their time optimizing things that no longer matter. Sometimes a feature, a process, or even an entire product category exists because it once solved an important problem. But when the environment changes, we should reconsider whether it still deserves our resources.

I don't expect today's organizational advantages, or my own advantages, to last forever. The question is whether we can keep building as those advantages change.

## VIII. Corporate Monarchism

I am interested in corporate monarchism because I think a strong center of strategic authority can be useful when building something new.

A founder's job is not always the same as a CEO's job. A CEO may be responsible for operations, execution, and management. A founder may also define why the organization exists, what it should become, whom it should recruit, and which principles should survive individual product cycles.

These responsibilities often overlap in an early-stage company. As the organization grows, they become more distinct.

I am especially interested in the founding function: turning an individual's vision into a durable institution.

This is why I admire Augustus. He accumulated personal power, but he also helped construct a political order that outlived him. The Roman Principate had contradictions and instability. Succession remained a persistent problem, and concentrated authority created dangers of its own. But I think the historical achievement is difficult to ignore.

Augustus helped turn a particular configuration of personal, military, and political power into a system that could continue beyond his lifetime. Winning power and making it durable are different problems.

But Augustus also shows the difficulty of transferring personal authority. His succession arrangements helped Tiberius become the next emperor, yet they did not establish a fully predictable system of imperial succession. The new ruler still had to establish his own authority within the political structures Augustus had created.

Building an institution that survives its founder and establishing a reliable system of succession are different problems.

![Augustus of Prima Porta, a Roman marble statue](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Augustus_of_Prima_Porta.jpg?width=800)

*Augustus of Prima Porta. Photograph by Justin Benttinen, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Augustus_of_Prima_Porta.jpg).*

Yarvin's Neocameralism is a modern attempt to think about political organization in corporate terms. His model separates ownership from daily management. Shareholders select a board, and the board appoints professional managers.

I think this is an important distinction. But ownership and management incentives alone cannot explain what makes an organization capable of creating something new. A good manager may preserve an existing system without being able to build the next one. This is why I place more emphasis on cognition, agency, and the founding function.

A person can own most of a company's shares and still be terrible at running it. A brilliant engineer may be unable to manage an organization. An effective CEO may be unable to define its long-term vision. These roles require different kinds of judgment.

My ideal organization is a **limited-open community of creators**. It is limited because I don't believe everyone should participate in every important decision. It is open because it should continuously discover and incorporate exceptional people.

I want a strong founder and people who can disagree with him for good reasons. Executives need meaningful authority over their responsibilities. Researchers and engineers need to be willing to say that an idea does not work. If everyone is afraid to disagree with the founder, the organization cannot make full use of the intelligence it has recruited.

The difficult question is where final authority should sit. A company cannot resolve every strategic disagreement through endless discussion. Someone eventually has to decide. I am comfortable with concentrated decision-making authority. But it needs institutions that allow accurate information, independent judgment, and correction.

Institutions can become more than instruments for one person's private preferences. This is one of the questions that interests me in Hegel's political philosophy. I want to build an organization that can sustain collective activity beyond an individual's lifetime or immediate intentions. It should be able to create, adapt, and act when I am no longer making every decision.

Succession is therefore one of the most important problems in corporate monarchism.

A founder may establish an institution, but that does not mean the founder can decide every future leadership question forever. At some point, authority must be transferred. The question is how.

I think legitimate succession is one of the most important distinctions between a recognized monarch and someone who simply seizes power. Both may possess power, but the way that power is acquired and transferred matters for the stability of an institution.

A durable organization needs a process through which authority can be recognized and transferred, rather than relying entirely on whoever happens to be strongest at a particular moment.

Succession should be based on capability and institutional recognition rather than bloodline. A founder may identify and appoint a potential successor, but the organization still needs a way to evaluate that person's abilities and establish the conditions of the transfer.

A mechanism for replacing incapable leaders can make concentrated authority more durable. It also gives the organization a way to correct a succession decision that turns out to be wrong.

A successor may develop a better strategy, or the founding vision may stop matching reality. If I value competence, I have to accept that someone may surpass my own.

This also applies to the relationship between the founder and the organization itself. Suppose I build an organization that eventually becomes more capable than me. Should I still have the right to rule it?

I may have a strong desire to rule it, but I know that it might no longer be mine to rule. Founders establish organizations and shape their institutional culture. But circumstances change, and our decisions need to change accordingly, just as a function produces a different output when it gets a new input.

I may eventually have to give up some or even all of my authority if the organization can operate better without me. I don't necessarily want to do that today. But I cannot turn my current position into an eternal principle.

Preserving an institution and preserving the personal supremacy of its founder are different objectives. I care more about building something that lasts than about ignoring that difference.

## IX. The End of Human Monopoly

Let me return to the thought experiment about automated production.

Suppose AI eventually performs most economically useful cognitive labor. Suppose robots can handle most physical production and maintenance, and automated industrial systems can increasingly reproduce the infrastructure they need to operate. Suppose some organizations also gain enough control over energy, computation, materials, and manufacturing to depend much less on external labor markets.

I think this would raise fundamental political questions. We have spent centuries building institutions for a world in which human beings remain central to production, consumption, administration, and coercive power. This is one reason large populations have political leverage.

But their central position may not be permanent.

If human labor becomes less economically necessary, some traditional bargaining mechanisms may weaken. If production and much of its consumption take place inside highly automated systems, external consumer demand may matter less to those particular organizations. If autonomous systems perform more administrative and security functions, political authority may also depend less on human participation.

The engineering obstacles are enormous. A self-sustaining industrial system is not a chatbot connected to a robotic arm. It needs reliable energy, resource extraction, precision manufacturing, maintenance, logistics, scientific capability, and ways to respond to unexpected failures.

Even a highly autonomous system would remain constrained by physics, its supply chains, and competition with other systems.

Political conflict would also continue. A community of a thousand creators can disagree about ownership, leadership, resource allocation, and succession. A society governed by AI would still face questions about who controls that intelligence, how its objectives are determined, and what happens when objectives diverge.

Fewer participants do not eliminate politics. I simply don't regard universal participation as a necessary objective.

This is where my thinking differs most clearly from Andreessen's. He imagines technology creating extraordinary abundance for humanity. I find that vision attractive, and I think many of its economic assumptions deserve serious consideration. But I don't believe every future civilization must remain human-centered.

Land is more willing to imagine technological processes moving beyond human purposes. I take that possibility seriously too. But acknowledging it does not require me to become a passive observer. I am a builder within evolution. I cannot control the whole process, but I want to influence its trajectory while I can.

There is one more consequence of this argument that I think is important.

If AI allows me to build an organization that no longer depends on ordinary people, the same technology may eventually allow that organization, its successors, or even its own agents to operate without me.

This independence works in both directions.

I may build an organization that becomes more capable than its founder. A successor may have better judgment than I do. An AI system may eventually perform functions that once required the founder's personal involvement. The organization may no longer need me to make decisions or even to define its direction.

**I may become unnecessary to something I created.**

I don't see this as a contradiction. It is simply another consequence of technological development. If I believe that intelligence and capability should be allowed to develop, I cannot demand that they stop developing when they become independent of me.

I want to create organizations that acquire new capabilities, build useful systems, and expand what intelligence can accomplish. I also accept that their development may eventually exceed my intentions.

Future AI systems may pursue scientific and civilizational objectives that humans cannot fully comprehend. New forms of coordination may replace our organizations and make today's companies look primitive. Future systems may no longer need human founders at all.

These are possible outcomes, not inevitable ones. But I don't want to build my philosophy on the assumption that my species, my organization, or I personally must remain supreme forever.

Hegel's image of the owl of Minerva taking flight at dusk is relevant here. We often understand a historical order only after it has matured, sometimes as it is beginning to pass away.

Maybe we will fully understand the political significance of human cognitive supremacy only when we can no longer take it for granted.

If that happens, I want to have participated in what comes next. I don't want to have spent that time only defending what came before.

## X. The Right to Build

The thinkers I have discussed help me understand different parts of the same problem.

Laozi makes me think about the underlying order of reality and the limits of intervention. Machiavelli makes me pay attention to the capability required to establish power. Mencius raises questions about the political importance of the people, while Hegel helps me think about institutions and their development through history.

Schumpeter explains how innovation can change economic structures. Hirschman provides a framework for reform and exit.

Thiel shows why building something new may require looking beyond existing political arrangements. Yarvin asks how political authority might be reorganized. Andreessen emphasizes the productive capacity of technology, and Land examines the processes through which technological development can move beyond individual human intentions.

What interests me is the relationship between intelligence, power, and construction.

Intelligence expands what we can understand, and technology expands what we can do. But having both does not mean that we can actually change the world. We still need power to turn our intentions into reality, and institutions to make sure that what we build can continue to exist beyond us.

I want to understand the rules that constrain reality and build as much as I can within them. I want to create technologies that improve the ability of intelligent systems to observe, reason, and act. I also want organizations that can turn those technologies into durable infrastructure and institutions.

I want more autonomy as a practical capability supported by energy, computation, production, and organizational competence. A legal declaration alone is not enough.

I want to accelerate the development of intelligence and civilization while recognizing that speed alone is not progress. And I want to participate in building a future that may eventually exceed what I can imagine today.

My actions are part of the causal structure of the world. I cannot stand outside causality and change my destiny from there. I can only act within it.

That is why I think action matters.

**I cannot command the universe to become what I desire. But I can become one of the causes through which it becomes something new.**

That is enough reason to build.

I want to accept the constraints, acquire the capability, and construct the future. I also have to accept the possibility of being surpassed.
